State of the Art: Accessible Learning for Visually Impaired Students

1. Existing Solutions and Research

Assistive technologies such as screen readers, text-to-speech (TTS) systems, and accessible digital documents help visually impaired students access educational materials. However, accessibility remains a challenge due to poorly structured PDFs, missing alternative text, and incompatibility with assistive technologies.

Kerdar, Bächler, and Kirchhoff (2024), in their scoping review on digital accessibility, identified several barriers affecting people with visual impairments. Similarly, Lomellini et al. (2025), in a review of accessible online learning in higher education, highlighted the need for inclusive educational content and better compatibility with assistive technologies.

2. Artificial Intelligence in Education

Artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities for accessible learning. AI can summarize lengthy documents and generate flashcards containing questions and answers, helping students review important concepts more efficiently. Combined with text-to-speech technology, these features allow visually impaired learners to listen to educational content and study without relying on visual interfaces.

However, AI-generated content may contain errors or omit important information. Therefore, summaries and flashcards should remain grounded in the original document and be checked for accuracy.

3. Proposed Approach and Evaluation

An AI-powered educational application for visually impaired students can improve access to learning materials by transforming PDF documents into accessible summaries, generating interactive flashcards, and enabling students to interact with educational content using voice commands.
The application should be designed around four fundamental principles:
1. Accessibility: Students should be able to navigate and use the application without relying on vision.
2. Educational effectiveness: AI-generated summaries and flashcards should help students understand, memorize, and review educational material.
3. Accuracy and reliability: AI-generated information must be consistent with the original learning materials.
4. Usability: The application should be intuitive, efficient, and comfortable for students with different levels of visual impairment and technical experience.

Approach 1: User-centered and participatory design
The application should be developed with direct involvement from visually impaired students, teachers, and accessibility specialists.
Key activities include interviewing students about learning challenges, observing how they use screen readers, creating accessible prototypes, and refining features based on feedback.
Students who are blind and students with low vision may have different requirements. For example, some may prefer audio navigation, while others may benefit from magnification, high contrast, and adjustable text sizes.

Approach 2: AI-based PDF summarization
Recommended techniques:
- Use extractive summarization for selecting important passages and abstractive summarization for simpler explanations.
- Use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to ground generated content in extracted document passages.
- Preserve page numbers and headings so students can navigate back to the original source.
- Detect missing text, unreadable scanned pages, and poorly extracted tables.
- Provide different summary lengths: short, medium, and detailed.

Approach 3: AI-generated flashcards
The flashcard module should convert educational content into structured question-and-answer pairs.
Flashcards should support audio playback, keyboard navigation, difficulty levels, spaced repetition, and student-controlled editing.
Generated answers should be validated against source passages before they are included in a study set.

Approach 4: Voice-based interaction
Voice interaction can combine speech recognition, intent recognition, and speech synthesis.
A student could say:
- "Summarize chapter three."
- "Read the next paragraph."
- "Generate five flashcards."
- "Repeat the question."
- "Explain this concept in simpler language."
The system processes the spoken instruction, identifies the intended action, retrieves the appropriate content, and provides an audio response.
Voice functionality should include adjustable speech speed, pause and resume controls, confirmation for destructive actions, and recovery from misunderstood commands.
Importantly, voice must be an alternative interaction method, not the only method. All essential functions should also work through screen readers and keyboards.

4. Existing Assistive Software and Educational Tools

Assistive software and educational tools for visually impaired students span several categories—from foundational operating system screen readers to specialized digital libraries and learning platforms. The primary tools available in this space include:

a. Screen Readers & Core Navigation Software
Screen readers convert text, menus, and UI elements into synthesized speech or refreshable Braille output, acting as the primary gateway to computers and mobile devices.
- JAWS (Job Access With Speech): The most widely used commercial screen reader for Windows. It offers advanced web scripting, deep enterprise and LMS (Canvas, Blackboard) integration, and robust support for Braille displays.
- NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access):  A free, open-source screen reader for Windows. Powered by a global community, it provides robust speech and Braille support and serves as a primary tool for students and schools with limited budgets.
- VoiceOver: Apple’s built-in, gesture-driven screen reader integrated directly into macOS, iOS, and iPadOS. It is heavily utilized in education due to the prevalence of iPads and Macbooks in modern classrooms.

b. Spaced Repetition & Flashcard Platforms
Anki: A powerful, open-source spaced repetition flashcard program widely used by university students for long-term memorization. While immensely popular, its default accessibility for blind users heavily depends on screen-reader keyboard shortcuts, third-party add-ons (like AnkiAccessibility), and platform-specific quirks—making custom, native accessibility a major design hurdle for students with visual impairments.

c. Accessible Libraries & Textbook Repositories
Because standard textbooks are often locked in inaccessible PDFs or physical print, specialized digital libraries format textbooks into accessible digital text, structured audio, and Braille.
- Bookshare: A massive global digital library for individuals with print disabilities. It provides textbooks, novels, and academic papers in accessible formats like structured EPUB, DAISY, and ready-to-print Braille files.
- Learning Ally: An educational library specializing in human-narrated audiobooks tailored for students with print and visual disabilities, offering synchronized text-and-audio features.
- DAISY Consortium Formats: An international standard for digital talking books, allowing users to navigate complex academic textbooks by chapter, section, page number, and footnote.

d. Specialized Educational Platforms & Apps
- Accessibyte: An accessible online learning suite specifically built for blind and visually impaired students. It features custom-designed apps for typing, learning, and practicing curriculum topics with high-contrast visuals, large text, and built-in screen reading.
- Voice Dream Reader: A popular mobile and desktop reading app designed for print-disabled learners. It turns documents, PDFs, and eBooks into high-quality spoken audio with advanced speed controls, synchronized highlighting, and custom formatting.

Integrating similar capabilities into one accessible application could reduce the need to switch between multiple tools when studying.
a. From Anki (Spaced Repetition & FSRS)
What to use: Adopt FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) as your underlying scheduling engine (via libraries like ts-fsrs), rather than inventing your own algorithm or using older models like SM-2.
Why it helps: Anki's community has proven that data-driven scheduling works, but Anki itself is notoriously difficult for blind users to navigate natively. Building FSRS into an accessibility-first voice interface gives you the pedagogical power of Anki without the accessibility barriers.

b. From NVDA & VoiceOver (Screen Reader Patterns)
What to use: Study how these platform screen readers handle keyboard-only navigation, landmark roles, and ARIA live regions, and incorporate those patterns into your Next.js frontend using React Aria Components.
Why it helps: You don't need to write a screen reader from scratch, but your app must respect them. Designing your UI primitives to cooperate with (or step aside for) native screen readers prevents the audio "cacophony" issue where the app's voice and the user's screen reader talk over each other simultaneously. 



5. Accessibility Standards and Evaluation Methods

Accessibility standards provide guidelines for designing digital applications that can be used by people with different disabilities. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, developed by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), organize accessibility requirements around four principles: perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. For an educational application designed for visually impaired students, relevant requirements include keyboard accessibility, sufficient color contrast, meaningful heading structures, descriptive labels for interactive elements, and compatibility with screen readers. These guidelines help developers identify potential barriers and establish measurable accessibility requirements.

Accessibility evaluation should combine automated testing with manual verification. Tools such as WAVE and axe-core can identify common accessibility issues, including missing accessible names, certain color contrast problems, and incorrect use of accessibility attributes. WAVE provides visual feedback about potential issues on web pages, while axe-core can be integrated into automated testing workflows. However, automated tools cannot identify every accessibility problem or determine whether an application is easy to use in practice. Manual testing is therefore necessary to verify keyboard navigation, logical focus order, screen reader announcements, and the accessibility of interactive components.

For the proposed application, accessibility testing should cover the main learning activities, including uploading PDF documents, listening to extracted text, accessing AI-generated summaries, and navigating flashcards. Each activity should be evaluated to determine whether it can be completed independently using a keyboard and a screen reader, such as NVDA. Additional checks should verify that text remains readable when enlarged, interface elements have clear labels, and feedback messages are accessible to assistive technologies. These tests can reveal usability barriers that automated evaluation may overlook.

Evaluation results can be documented using a checklist based on relevant WCAG success criteria. Useful measures include the number and severity of accessibility issues identified, the proportion of essential tasks completed successfully, and the difficulties encountered during testing. Where feasible, involving visually impaired students in usability studies can provide valuable feedback about navigation, clarity, and independence. Combining standards-based evaluation, automated tools, manual testing, and user feedback provides a more comprehensive assessment of the application's accessibility and helps guide future improvements.
